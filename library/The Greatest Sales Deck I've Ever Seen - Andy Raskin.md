> [!summary] Principles
> - **Positioning becomes persuasive when it is told as a story.** Raskin breaks a strong B2B narrative into five elements, used in this order.
> - **1. Name a big, relevant change in the world.** Don't open with your company or with "the problem": telling prospects they have a problem puts them on the defensive. A shift in the world gets them talking about stakes and opportunities.
> - **2. Show there will be winners and losers.** Buyers are loss-averse and default to the status quo, so show both the upside of adapting and the cost of not adapting.
> - **3. Tease the "Promised Land":** the future state the buyer wants, which is hard to reach alone. It is a state of the world, not your product.
> - **4. Introduce features as "magic gifts"** that overcome specific obstacles on the way there. Features shown before this context get tuned out.
> - **5. Present evidence** that you can make the story come true: customer stories first, demos next.
> - **The narrative works best when the whole company tells it.** Marketing campaigns and the CEO's talks act as air cover for sales.

> [!warning] Numbers and tactics ⏳ as of 2016-09
> - The example is Zuora's sales deck, built around the phrase "subscription economy" as its named shift. Zuora was then a private company preparing to go public.
> - Results are anecdotal: one salesperson using the structure reported faster openness from prospects and the largest deal in their company's history.
