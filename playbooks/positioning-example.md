# Positioning a B2B tech product

> [!info] A three-source example
> A real hub draws on every relevant canon piece plus the best of the web. This one uses three library notes, so it shows the format, not a complete answer.

## 1. The job

Decide how your product wins today: what buyers compare you with, what only you can do, why it matters, who cares most, and which market frame makes that obvious. Then turn it into a story the whole company tells.

**Typical length:** none of the three sources gives one. Treat it as a project followed by a standing review; in AI categories, revisit every few months (as of 2026-05) ([[AI-Powered Isn't a Position - Arielle Jackson on First Round Review|Arielle Jackson]]).

## 2. What success looks like

- [ ] The category name makes buyers assume true things about competitors, features, buyer and price ([[A Quickstart Guide to Positioning - April Dunford|April Dunford]])
- [ ] You can name the alternative you beat most often, including "do nothing" ([[A Quickstart Guide to Positioning - April Dunford|April Dunford]])
- [ ] Your differences are visible to buyers, not just real ([[AI-Powered Isn't a Position - Arielle Jackson on First Round Review|Arielle Jackson]])
- [ ] Sales, marketing and the CEO tell the same story ([[The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen - Andy Raskin|Andy Raskin]])

**Mid-cycle test:** swap your logo for a competitor's on your homepage. If the page still makes sense, the positioning isn't showing yet ([[AI-Powered Isn't a Position - Arielle Jackson on First Round Review|Arielle Jackson]]).

### Where the sources agree

Only two practices are backed by all three sources:

1. **Position against the status quo, not only named rivals.** Buyers default to what they do today, so that is the first thing to beat ([[A Quickstart Guide to Positioning - April Dunford|April Dunford]]; [[AI-Powered Isn't a Position - Arielle Jackson on First Round Review|Arielle Jackson]]; [[The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen - Andy Raskin|Andy Raskin]]).
2. **Set the context before the features.** Value before features, "why care" before differences, the destination before the "magic gifts" ([[A Quickstart Guide to Positioning - April Dunford|April Dunford]]; [[AI-Powered Isn't a Position - Arielle Jackson on First Round Review|Arielle Jackson]]; [[The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen - Andy Raskin|Andy Raskin]]).

## 3. What changes the answer

| If the situation is… | then size the process like… |
|---|---|
| A few customers love the product | Start from them: what they'd do without you, and why they stay ([[A Quickstart Guide to Positioning - April Dunford\|April Dunford]]) |
| An incumbent owns your category | Narrow the category to a segment you clearly win, e.g. "CRM for investment banks" ([[A Quickstart Guide to Positioning - April Dunford\|April Dunford]]) |
| Crowded AI category, features copied fast | Lead with a point of view and distinctive brand; revisit every few months (as of 2026-05) ([[AI-Powered Isn't a Position - Arielle Jackson on First Round Review\|Arielle Jackson]]) |
| Buyers agree you're better but don't act | Fix the narrative: name the shift and the cost of standing still ([[The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen - Andy Raskin\|Andy Raskin]]) |
| Tempted to create a new category | Default to an existing one, narrowed; creating one is slow and costly ([[A Quickstart Guide to Positioning - April Dunford\|April Dunford]]) |

## 4. The process

**Phase 1: Learn from best-fit customers.** Dunford's "failed" product was saved by the 6 enthusiasts among 100 buyers called ([[A Quickstart Guide to Positioning - April Dunford|April Dunford]]).
- [ ] Interview the customers who get the most value
- [ ] Record what they'd use if you didn't exist

**Phase 2: Work the five components in order:** alternatives → differentiated capabilities → value → best-fit customers → market category ([[A Quickstart Guide to Positioning - April Dunford|April Dunford]]).
- [ ] Alternatives come from real deals, with no phantom competitors
- [ ] Every capability has a "so what" for customers
- [ ] The segment is who cares most, not who could buy

**Phase 3: Check it's legible and opinionated** ([[AI-Powered Isn't a Position - Arielle Jackson on First Round Review|Arielle Jackson]]).
- [ ] Homepage hero, a customer email and a social post share one point of view
- [ ] The logo-swap test fails

**Phase 4: Turn it into a narrative:** shift → winners and losers → Promised Land → features as gifts → evidence ([[The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen - Andy Raskin|Andy Raskin]]).
- [ ] The first slide names a change in the world, not your company

**Phase 5: Reinforce and revisit** across every channel as the market moves ([[AI-Powered Isn't a Position - Arielle Jackson on First Round Review|Arielle Jackson]]; [[The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen - Andy Raskin|Andy Raskin]]).

**Lightweight version:** one working session on the five components, then the logo-swap test.

## 6. Where the sources disagree

- **Where to start.** Dunford starts with competitive alternatives, Jackson with a point of view, Raskin with a change in the world. *When each applies:* Dunford's order finds the position, Jackson's POV keeps it distinctive as products converge, Raskin's shift is how you tell it. Layers, not rivals.
- **How long a position lasts.** Dunford describes planned steps as you expand (banks, then financial services); Jackson says AI categories need revisiting every few months (as of 2026-05). *When each applies:* slow categories hold a position longer than fast-copying AI markets.
- **Naming something new.** Dunford warns category creators often lose to later entrants; Raskin praises Zuora for coining "subscription economy". *When each applies:* naming a shift in the world is cheaper than teaching a new product category.

## 7. Failure modes

- Positioning against competitors buyers never consider, filling in a positioning statement as if each blank had one answer, or describing value as "anything you want" ([[A Quickstart Guide to Positioning - April Dunford|April Dunford]])
- "AI-powered X" as the whole position, and copy hedged to offend no one ([[AI-Powered Isn't a Position - Arielle Jackson on First Round Review|Arielle Jackson]])
- Opening the pitch with your company, or telling prospects they have a problem ([[The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen - Andy Raskin|Andy Raskin]])
